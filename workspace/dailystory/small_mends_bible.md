## 1. Series identity

- **series_title:** `Small Mends`
- **series_key:** `small_mends`
- **length:** 30 chapters, one per day
- **house standard to match:** `workspace/dailystory/the_long_room.json`

**Pitch.** Alison Hoyle spent twenty-six years in the alterations room on the fourth floor of Ellerby's department store in Ravensgate, taking up other women's hems under a north window, and when the store closes she is fifty-two years old with a Singer 201K, a redundancy cheque that works out at about eight hundred and thirty pounds for each year she gave them, and no idea what she is now. She takes a mending table in the Ravensgate market hall — a glass-roofed Victorian barn the traders call the Lantern — in the worst of the draught and the best of the light. Every day a garment arrives, and a life arrives with it: a knee in a boy's school trousers, a suit bought in 2009 for a job that never came, a dress a woman has been driving round in the boot of her car for a year because she cannot fasten it. Alison mends them. The women of the hall become, without anybody announcing it, a place to belong. And while she is letting out other people's seams her own body is quietly altering itself without a fitting — nobody chalked the line, nobody asked her to stand still. Over eight autumn weeks the hall's future comes into question and is answered communally, a friendship that went silent in April is repaired, and on the last Saturday before the clocks go back, Alison Hoyle — who has spent a working lifetime adjusting things other people made — cuts and makes a coat of her own.

## 2. Voice notes

Distilled from *The Long Room*. Read chapters 1, 15, 29 and 30 of `the_long_room.json` before drafting. Then:

- **Close third, past tense, unhurried, Northern.** Long precise sentences that do not rush to the feeling. Let concrete objects carry the emotion — the brass yard-measure worn to a shine at thirty-six inches, the masking-tape name on a workroom hook, the biro behind Bernie's ear that she never writes with. If a paragraph states a feeling outright, cut the statement and give the reader the object instead.
- **Emotional restraint; warmth earned, never claimed.** Nobody makes a speech. The big things are said flatly and sideways, often while someone is doing something with their hands. Dry Northern humour is welcome and is a form of tenderness. Never twee, never inspirational, never a moral at the end of the chapter.
- **Midlife is texture, not subject.** The four o'clock waking, the heat arriving mid-morning at the table, the needle-eye that now wants more light, the thumb joints stiff in the cold. It is *woven in* the way weather is woven in. No clinical language, no explaining, no lesson. The single permitted medical object across the whole series is a small clear patch on her hip, noticed once, in chapter 29, and not discussed.
- **Calm and safe throughout.** No gore, no on-page violence, no medical peril, no cruelty, no jeopardy to children, no villains, no bleak endings. Conflict is money, silence, a lease, a body, and time.
- **UK spelling and idiom. No emoji. No brand names except real, plain ones** (Boots, the Co-op, the Health Centre, Argos). Northern speech is rendered lightly — *owt*, *nowt*, *love*, *give over* — in dialogue only, never in narration.

**Calibration paragraph (60 words). This is the finished voice:**

> The heat came at half past ten, the way it did now, arriving without knocking. Alison put the needle down before it reached her hands, because a needle does not care what your body is doing. She opened the north door two inches. Bernie, four stalls down, did not look up, and did not stop talking, and slid the little fan along the counter towards her.

## 3. Cast

### The place

**Ravensgate** — a mill town in the Calder Valley, West Yorkshire, between Halifax and Todmorden. Stone gone black then washed back to honey, a beck under the main street, a hill at the end of every road. Trains to Leeds and Manchester. Two chip shops, a Turkish barber, three charity shops and a Boots.

**Ravensgate Market Hall**, built 1877, iron and glass, a barrel roof with eleven glazed lanterns down its spine — which is why nobody in the town has called it the Market Hall since about 1950. It is **the Lantern**. Thirty-one pitches, twenty-two of them let. Cast-iron columns painted a green that has been touched up in four different greens. It smells of pastry, cut keys, cardboard and cold stone. Trades Monday to Saturday; Wednesday is half-day; closed Sundays. Alison's table stands by the **north door**, where the draught is worst and the light is best.

**Ellerby's** — the department store on Corn Street, founded 1904, four floors, closed for good at half past five on Tuesday 1 September 2026 after 122 years. The alterations room was on the fourth floor at the back, with a north window.

### Alison Hoyle, 52

Born 1974 in Ravensgate, and never left it, which she has been asked to account for more than once. Trained at Ravensgate Technical College at sixteen; two years at a curtain workshop; six at a bridal shop in Halifax; then Ellerby's from March 2000 — twenty-six years of other people's hems, other people's zips, other people's daughters standing on a box in front of a three-way mirror. Married Gary Nuttall in 2001, divorced him in 2015, and speaks of him about twice a year without heat. One son, Joe, twenty-three, in Glasgow. She is extremely good at a thing the world has decided is not a profession. **What she wants:** to be worth what she is worth, and to be asked, once, to make something rather than fix it. **What she is avoiding:** her own measurements. She has not taken them in years. She has not made a garment of her own since she was nineteen. She has spent her life leaving seam allowance for other women's bodies and none at all for her own. **Concrete detail:** the pad of her right thumb is worn smooth and slightly flat from thirty-six years of pushing a needle, and she rubs it against her forefinger when she is thinking.

### Sylvia Hoyle, 79

Her mother. Alive, sharp, ungovernable, in a warden-assisted flat on Weaver's Rise with a view of the crematorium she describes as *handy*. Plays bowls on Tuesdays; has been on the committee and been asked to leave the committee. Taught Alison to sew badly, which Alison has never once mentioned. **Detail:** a beige raincoat bought in 1988 from a shop that no longer exists, worn to the weight of paper, which she will not replace and will not discuss.

### Joe Hoyle, 23

Assistant to a sound engineer in Glasgow. Rings on Sundays, mostly. Sends photographs of his dinners with no message attached. Loves his mother in the way of a young man who does not yet know he will one day want to say so. **Detail:** he still cannot sew a button and posts things four hundred miles to avoid learning.

### Pauline Craddock, 53

Nineteen years beside Alison in the Ellerby's workroom. Since the fourteenth of April they have not spoken, and neither has rung. Pauline sat on the staff consultation panel and knew things in January she could not say in February, and Alison found out in April with everyone else, and something went quiet that neither woman has known how to make a noise in again. Not a betrayal. A silence, which is harder. **Detail:** left-handed scissors with a red tape round the handle, which she never let anybody borrow, not once, in nineteen years.

### Sonia Aktar, 38

Aktar's Trimmings, pitch 4, since 1979 — her father's shop, and his father's counter. Zips, bias binding, buttons in drawers labelled in two alphabets. Watches everything and says roughly a third of it. **Detail:** a brass yard-measure screwed to the counter, worn to a shine at thirty-six inches by seventy years of thumbs.

### Bernie Fazakerley, 66

Fazakerley's pies, pitch 11. Liverpool-born, Ravensgate-married, forty years in the hall and a running commentary on all of them. Refuses money from anybody in their first week. **Detail:** a biro behind her ear that she never writes with; she writes with a different one, from her apron.

### Yusuf Barzani, 44

Keys cut, shoes repaired, pitch 19. Came from Kurdistan via Sheffield in 2008; his English is better than he lets on because being underestimated is restful. **Detail:** he can tell from the sound of the wheel whether a key will turn before he hands it over, and he is never wrong.

### Margo Denby, 74

Records and sheet music, pitch 2. Sang in a band in 1971 for eleven months and will tell you about it if you are unlucky. Widowed, unbothered, magnificent. **Detail:** green nail varnish, always chipped on the left thumb.

### Kelly Aspinall, 29

Coffee cart at the north door — Alison's nearest neighbour and the reason her hands are warm. **Detail:** the chalkboard says CAMOMILE spelled wrong, and she knows, and she is not changing it.

### Hilary Ashworth, 58

Superintendent of the hall. Carries the lease, the leak, the fire door and everybody's business. **Detail:** thirty-one keys on a ring, three of which open nothing at all, and she cannot bring herself to take them off.

### Ivan Prewitt, 41

Council officer, assets and regeneration. Decent, tired, bad at rooms. Arrives looking like the enemy and turns out to be a man with a list of what is genuinely possible and no power to make any of it happen unless the traders do it themselves. **Not a villain. There are no villains in this series.**

## 4. The arc in four movements

- **Chapters 1–7 — The end of the north window.** Ellerby's closes and takes twenty-six years with it; Alison works out what she is worth on paper, takes a table nobody has wanted for two years, mends her first knee for four pounds, and discovers that the hall has been watching her all along — and that a dress with somebody else's chalk mark inside it can name a silence she has been carrying since April.
- **Chapters 8–14 — The hall takes her in.** Barter, pies, a wedding dress with no name on it, an interview suit, curtains she said she did not do, and a first Saturday queue: she stops being a woman with a table and becomes part of a place — just as an envelope lands on Hilary's desk that has been sitting unopened for six days.
- **Chapters 15–22 — What there is enough of.** At the midpoint a woman brings in a dress she cannot fasten, and Alison finds the inch and a half the original maker left in it, and understands what she has been doing with her life; then the hall's future goes to a meeting, a friendship is repaired with a stick of tailor's chalk, and the community stops waiting to be rescued.
- **Chapters 23–30 — Made, not mended.** Six women, six holes and a daylight lamp on Thursday evenings; a jumper that cannot be saved and is made into something else; a raincoat relined; the hall kept; and on the last Saturday before the clocks go back, a woman who has spent her life altering what other people made cuts four yards of cloth to her own measurements and walks out of the north door in a coat.

**1 — Half Past Five** · Tue 1 Sep
*Garment:* a navy wool coat, brought up to the fourth floor at ten to five on the last day by a woman who has not understood that the store is closing forever, to be taken up an inch and a half.
*Whose life:* nobody's, and everybody's — the last customer Ellerby's ever has.
*What shifts:* twenty-six years end. Alison does the job for nothing, in forty minutes, on a machine that has already been sold and has a lot number stuck to its side. She peels the strip of masking tape off her hook — HOYLE, written in 2001 in her own hand — and puts it in her pocket.
*Closing image:* she turns off the light over the bench and the room stays bright, because it is a north window, and the light was never the bulb.
*Cliffhanger:* `There is a table standing empty in the Lantern that nobody has wanted for two years.`

**2 — What She Was Worth** · Wed 2 Sep
*Object:* the redundancy letter, and the Singer 201K on her own kitchen table where the bread board goes.
*Whose life:* hers, and money's.
*What shifts:* twenty-one thousand five hundred and seventy pounds. She does the division on the back of the envelope without meaning to and gets about eight hundred and thirty pounds for each year she gave them, and then she is annoyed with herself for doing the division. Sylvia rings to ask what she is going to do now. She says she is having a think.
*Closing image:* the machine sitting on the kitchen table at eleven at night, plugged into nothing, and Alison in the doorway looking at it the way you look at a horse in a hallway.
*Cliffhanger:* `The table is under the worst of the draught and the best of the light.`

**3 — The Lantern** · Thu 3 Sep
*Object:* the table itself — deal, scarred, a ghost sign still on its front board: T. CRABTREE & SON · NOTHING OVER 50p.
*Whose life:* the hall's. Hilary, thirty-one keys, the leak over pitch 7, seventy-two pounds a week all in.
*What shifts:* she says yes before she has decided to. She scrubs the table and the water comes off the colour of tea, twice.
*Closing image:* she stands behind it at four in the afternoon with nothing to do, under eleven glass lanterns, being looked at by a hall full of people who have all already decided how long she will last.
*Cliffhanger:* `The first customer comes at nine minutes past ten, and she is not ready.`

**4 — A Knee** · Fri 4 Sep
*Garment:* a boy's school trousers, both knees, six weeks into the term.
*Whose life:* a young mother, twenty-six, uniform money gone, who has been holding the trousers in a Co-op bag since Tuesday and rehearsing what to say.
*What shifts:* Alison charges four pounds because she cannot think of a number and four pounds is the first number that arrives. She hates herself for it for an hour and then does not.
*Closing image:* the woman leaving with the bag, and stopping at the north door, and looking back at the table as though checking it will still be there on Monday.
*Cliffhanger:* `The woman at the trimmings stall has watched her all morning and not said a word.`

**5 — The Zip** · Sat 5 Sep
*Garment:* a leather biker jacket, 1994, zip gone at the bottom stop.
*Whose life:* a woman of sixty-three who has started riding again after thirty-one years, and has told her son and not her daughter.
*What shifts:* joy arrives at the table and Alison is not expecting it. She has to send out to Sonia for a heavy-gauge zip, which means crossing the hall, which means speaking to Sonia.
*Closing image:* the brass yard-measure on Sonia's counter, worn to a shine at thirty-six inches, and Sonia's thumb landing on the shine without looking, the way a thumb does after seventy years.
*Cliffhanger:* `Bernie Fazakerley does not take money off anybody in their first week.`

**6 — Her Mother's Raincoat** · Sun 6 Sep
*Garment:* Sylvia's beige raincoat, 1988, worn to the weight of paper, cuffs gone through.
*Whose life:* Sylvia's — bowls, the committee, the crematorium view, seventy-nine years of not being asked.
*What shifts:* Alison offers to buy her a new one and is refused with a completeness that shuts the subject like a door. She takes the coat home to mend the cuffs and does not mend them.
*Closing image:* the raincoat over the back of a kitchen chair all evening, holding the shape of a woman who is not in it.
*Cliffhanger:* `Her mother would like to know what she is now, and will keep asking until she is told.`

**7 — Someone Else's Chalk** · Tue 8 Sep
*Garment:* a dress with an Ellerby's label, brought in to be taken in — and inside the side seam, a chalk mark from 2019 in a hand that is not Alison's.
*Whose life:* Pauline Craddock's, in absentia. Nineteen years side by side. Alison would know that hand at fifty paces.
*What shifts:* the silence since April acquires a shape and a date. She works the whole afternoon with another woman's chalk line under her fingers and does not rub it out.
*Closing image:* the chalk mark surviving the alteration, folded now into the inside of the new seam, where nobody will ever see it again.
*Cliffhanger:* `She has not spoken to Pauline Craddock since the fourteenth of April, and Pauline has not rung either.`

**8 — Bernie's Pocket** · Thu 10 Sep
*Garment:* Bernie's tabard, the right pocket torn clean off by forty years of hands.
*Whose life:* Bernie's — Liverpool, the hall, four decades of pastry and commentary.
*What shifts:* barter. Alison rebuilds the pocket and doubles it and Bernie will not have money named in her presence; Alison eats a steak pie standing up at eleven in the morning and it is the first hot food anyone has given her since April. The biro is behind Bernie's ear the entire time and never once comes down.
*Closing image:* the new pocket, deliberately made an inch deeper than the old one, and Bernie not noticing, and Bernie's hand going into it all afternoon as though it had always been that deep.
*Cliffhanger:* `Somebody has left a wedding dress on her table with no name attached to it.`

**9 — The Second Wearer** · Sat 12 Sep
*Garment:* a wedding dress from 1985, brought by a daughter to be let out for herself.
*Whose life:* a woman of thirty-four who wants her mother's dress and is frightened of what the wanting will cost her.
*What shifts:* Alison handles it as a technical problem, out loud, deliberately — *there's plenty here, they left plenty* — and the young woman's shoulders come down two inches. The tenderness is entirely in the competence. Not one word about the body.
*Closing image:* Alison holding the dress up to the north light to read the old maker's seam allowance through the cloth, and finding it generous.
*Cliffhanger:* `In the morning there is a woman waiting at the north door in a suit she bought in 2009.`

**10 — The Suit** · Mon 14 Sep
*Garment:* a black trouser suit, bought 2009, worn twice, interview on Thursday.
*Whose life:* a woman of forty-nine going back to work after eleven years out, who apologises four times in the first minute.
*What shifts:* Alison hears her own CV coming out of someone else's mouth. She takes the shoulders in and lifts the sleeve-head half an inch, which is a small thing and is not.
*Closing image:* the woman in front of Sonia's long mirror, standing up straighter without being asked to, and neither of them saying anything at all about it.
*Cliffhanger:* `Somebody is coming with curtains, and she has already said she does not do curtains.`

**11 — Curtains** · Wed 16 Sep
*Garment:* two pairs of lined curtains, drop too long, a widower's front room.
*Whose life:* a man of seventy-one who has not moved anything in that room since March and has decided to start with the curtains.
*What shifts:* Alison learns what her no is made of, and that it is made of Ellerby's, where curtains were beneath the fourth floor. She does the curtains. She charges properly for the first time — thirty-eight pounds — and does not apologise for the number, and he pays it without blinking, and she is furious about all the years.
*Closing image:* six feet of lining fabric on the floor of the hall, and Kelly wordlessly moving the coffee cart eighteen inches so Alison can lay it flat.
*Cliffhanger:* `The lad with the football shirt will not say who signed it.`

**12 — The Shoulder** · Fri 18 Sep
*Garment:* a football shirt, signed across the shoulder in marker, seam split at the sleeve.
*Whose life:* a lad of nineteen and his grandad, who is not with him and is the reason he has come.
*What shifts:* pure joy, and a technical puzzle — how to close a seam without putting a needle through a signature. She does it. It takes fifty minutes and she charges five pounds and it is the best fifty minutes of her week.
*Closing image:* the lad photographing the mended shoulder in the north light to send to somebody, and turning the phone twice to get it right.
*Cliffhanger:* `On Saturday, for the first time, there is a queue.`

**13 — A Queue** · Sat 19 Sep
*Garment:* eleven of them, in bags, on the floor, on the table, on the back of the chair.
*Whose life:* the hall's. Word has gone round the way word goes round in a town of that size.
*What shifts:* she is a business. She is also, by two o'clock, wrecked, and the heat comes at half ten as usual and she opens the north door two inches and Bernie slides the little fan along the counter without looking up.
*Closing image:* the queue at five to four still four deep, and Hilary standing at the end of it with nothing in her hands.
*Cliffhanger:* `There is an envelope in Hilary's office that she has not opened for six days.`

**14 — The Envelope** · Tue 22 Sep
*Object:* an A4 envelope, a council asset review, and Ivan Prewitt's clipboard.
*Whose life:* everybody's. Twenty-two lets, thirty-one pitches, a roof, a lease with fourteen months on it and an operator interested in the ground floor.
*What shifts:* Alison has been trading for nineteen days and has just found somewhere to be, and the somewhere has a date on it. Ivan is not a villain; he is a man reading out numbers he did not write, badly, to a room of people whose lives are in them.
*Closing image:* Hilary's thirty-one keys on the desk between them, and three of them that open nothing.
*Cliffhanger:* `A woman is coming in the morning with a dress she cannot fasten, and it has been in the boot of her car for a year.`

**15 — Seam Allowance** · Thu 24 Sep — ★ *this one lands*
*Garment:* a green dress, bought for a day in 2019 that mattered, carried about in a car boot for a year because the woman could neither wear it nor let it go.
*Whose life:* a woman of fifty-five who says, lightly, the way you say a thing you have practised in the car, that she has ruined it.
*What shifts:* everything. Alison turns the dress inside out and finds an inch and a half of seam allowance folded in on both sides, put there by whoever made it, in a factory, at speed, for no reason except that this is what a maker does. She says it out loud, to the woman, in the plainest voice she owns: *they left it in on purpose — a maker leaves you room, because nobody's body is a monument.* And then, in the silence of her own head, standing under the eleven lanterns with the dress in her hands: her own body has been altering itself for two years without a fitting. Nobody chalked the line. Nobody asked her to stand still. She has left room for every woman in this town and none for herself.
*Closing image:* the dress fastened, on the woman, in the north light, and the woman not looking in the mirror at all but at Alison, and Alison — who has done this ten thousand times — having to turn away to the machine and find something to do with her hands.
*Cliffhanger:* `Sonia Aktar's father's shears have not been out of their cloth since 2011.`

**16 — The Yard Measure** · Sat 26 Sep
*Garment:* a wedding order too big for one pair of hands — six kameez, eleven days, Sonia's cousin's daughter.
*Whose life:* Sonia's, and her father's, whose shears come out of their cloth wrap for the first time in fifteen years and are still sharp because he sharpened them in 2011 knowing he would not use them again.
*What shifts:* Alison is asked to make, not mend, and finds she is frightened of it, and says yes anyway, and they work until seven with the hall dark around them and the two lamps on.
*Closing image:* two women at one counter at seven at night under the lanterns, and the brass measure between them worn to a shine at thirty-six inches by four generations of thumbs, two of which are now theirs.
*Cliffhanger:* `Margo Denby needs something to wear on Wednesday, and will not say what for.`

**17 — Margo's Green** · Mon 28 Sep
*Garment:* a green frock, 1971, worn on stage eleven months of one year, needing a good deal of letting out and one seam moved entirely.
*Whose life:* Margo's. The funeral is her old bass player's, in Rochdale, and she has decided to go in the frock, and everyone can think what they like.
*What shifts:* dry humour and grief in the same forty minutes, and Alison learns that you can be seventy-four and still be the most alive thing in a room. Margo tells one story that is nine-tenths lies and one-tenth the truth, and the truth is the bit she says quietest.
*Closing image:* the chipped green thumbnail against the green cloth, the two greens not matching in the slightest, and Margo saying she is aware.
*Cliffhanger:* `There is a blazer in the bag with a name tape in it that is not anybody's name any more.`

**18 — A Name Tape** · Wed 30 Sep
*Garment:* a school blazer, taken in at the waist, and one name tape to come off and one to go on.
*Whose life:* a fourteen-year-old and a mother who has brought them, and neither of whom needs anything from Alison except the job doing properly and no face pulling while it is done.
*What shifts:* Alison does what she has always done — she asks the only question that is hers to ask, which is what the new tape should say, and how they would like it spelled. Half-day, so the hall is quiet. No speeches. No debate. Warmth entirely in the craft.
*Closing image:* the old tape unpicked and handed to the mother, who puts it in her purse behind a card, and Alison seeing her do it and never mentioning it, then or ever.
*Cliffhanger:* `The Singer has been making a noise since Tuesday, and this morning it stops.`

**19 — The Motor** · Fri 2 Oct
*Object:* the Singer 201K, dead, and its motor, and Yusuf's hands.
*Whose life:* Yusuf's — Kurdistan, Sheffield, 2008, a workshop of somebody else's tools and being underestimated as a form of rest.
*What shifts:* the machine is her whole livelihood and she is, briefly, properly frightened about money for the first time in the series. Yusuf carries it to pitch 19, takes the motor apart on a sheet of newspaper, finds a carbon brush worn to nothing, and mends it for the price of the brush, which is one pound sixty.
*Closing image:* Yusuf listening to the machine's first run with his head on one side, the way he listens to a key on the wheel, and nodding before it has finished the seam.
*Cliffhanger:* `On Saturday the traders are meeting in the hall after closing, and everybody has been asked.`

**20 — A Show of Hands** · Sat 3 Oct
*Object:* twenty-two traders, forty chairs, one urn, Kelly's chalkboard turned round and used as a flip chart.
*Whose life:* the hall's. The fourteen months. The operator. The roof.
*What shifts:* the meeting is a mess, as meetings are — Bernie talks for nine minutes, Margo proposes something illegal, Hilary reads the lease aloud in a voice that gives nothing away — and out of the mess comes one sentence from Sonia that nobody argues with: *they're not taking it off us, they're waiting for us to not want it.*
*Closing image:* the hall at nine at night with the lanterns off and the streetlight coming through the glass instead, and twenty-two people not leaving.
*Cliffhanger:* `Pauline Craddock is at the north door at ten past nine, holding something she should not have.`

**21 — Left-Handed Scissors** · Tue 6 Oct — *the repair*
*Object:* a stick of Ellerby's tailor's chalk and the left-handed scissors with the red tape, both of which Pauline took out of the workroom on the last day, in her bag, which is theft, and which is not.
*Whose life:* Pauline's. She knew in January. The panel. The wording she was given. Eleven weeks of standing three feet from Alison and saying nothing about the only thing that mattered.
*What shifts:* the silence is spoken, badly, in short sentences, over a table, with both of them looking at the chalk instead of each other. Nobody apologises well. Nobody makes a speech. Pauline says she has been walking past the north door since the second week. Alison says she knows; Bernie told her; Bernie tells everybody everything.
*Closing image:* the scissors put down on Alison's table, handle towards her, by a woman who did not let anybody borrow them for nineteen years.
*Cliffhanger:* `Ivan Prewitt has a list of what is possible, and it is shorter than anybody hoped.`

**22 — What Is Possible** · Thu 8 Oct
*Object:* Ivan's list, four items, on a single sheet, read out badly in the hall's back office with the door open so people can hear.
*Whose life:* Ivan's, briefly and humanly — a man with no power to give them the thing and enough decency to tell them exactly how to take it: a community benefit society, a fifteen-year lease, a business plan by the twelfth, a chair and a secretary with names on a form.
*What shifts:* the hall stops waiting to be rescued. Alison, asked to be on it, says no — she has been trading for five weeks, she is the newest person in the building, she is not going to stand up in front of anybody.
*Closing image:* the form on the table, and the pen, and Alison walking to the north door with her coat half on.
*Cliffhanger:* `They have put her name down anyway.`

**23 — Six Needles** · Sat 10 Oct
*Object:* six women, six holes, a daylight lamp from Argos that cost twenty-nine pounds and is the best money she has ever spent.
*Whose life:* the beginning of the Thursday class — Kelly, the young mother from chapter 4, the woman with the green dress, two strangers, and Pauline, at the back, saying nothing and threading everybody's needles.
*What shifts:* she can teach. She did not know this. And the lamp means she can see the eye of the needle again at half past six, which is a thing she has told nobody she had stopped being able to do.
*Closing image:* six heads under one lamp in a dark hall, and the light of it going up through the glass lanterns into the rain, so that from Corn Street the roof of the Lantern is lit from inside.
*Cliffhanger:* `There is a jumper coming that nobody can save.`

**24 — What Cannot Be Saved** · Mon 12 Oct
*Garment:* a lambswool jumper, moth, past all mending — a widow's, her husband's, six years.
*Whose life:* a woman of sixty-eight who knows perfectly well it cannot be mended and has come anyway, and Alison is the first person to say so out loud and kindly.
*What shifts:* Alison learns the difference between a lie and a kindness. She says no, and then she says: *but there's four good inches at the ribbing and the cuffs are sound.* She makes two pairs of mittens out of a dead man's jumper. The first thing in the series that is made rather than mended. Restraint absolute; no crying on the page.
*Closing image:* the woman putting one mitten on in the hall, in October, indoors, and leaving it on.
*Cliffhanger:* `There is a parcel from Glasgow on the step, addressed in her son's handwriting.`

**25 — Glasgow, By Post** · Wed 14 Oct
*Garment:* Joe's jacket, sleeves, a job interview on Monday, posted four hundred miles because he still cannot sew a button and would rather pay the postage than learn.
*Whose life:* Joe's, and hers as a mother of a grown man — the Sunday phone calls, the photographs of dinners with no message.
*What shifts:* she does the sleeves in an hour and then sits with the jacket on her knee for another one. She sews a button on the inside of the pocket, spare, the way her own mother used to, and does not mention it in the note.
*Closing image:* the parcel taped up on the post office counter with the address in her handwriting now, going the other way.
*Cliffhanger:* `Her mother has decided the raincoat is done for, and Alison has decided otherwise.`

**26 — Relined** · Fri 16 Oct
*Garment:* the beige raincoat, 1988, which Sylvia has finally conceded is beyond it, and which Alison takes apart to the last seam and rebuilds — new lining, new cuffs, the outside untouched, the shape exactly as it was.
*Whose life:* Sylvia's, and a mother and daughter who have never once said the thing.
*What shifts:* Sylvia puts it on in her own hallway, and puts her hands in the pockets, and looks at herself for a long time in a mirror she has had since 1988, and says, to the mirror, not to Alison: *you've never made owt for yourself, our Alison. Twenty-six year and you've never made owt for yourself.* And will not take it back, and will not soften it, and puts the kettle on.
*Closing image:* Alison on the bus home, in the dark, at five o'clock, with the empty bag on her knee, doing sums that are not about money.
*Cliffhanger:* `On Saturday the hall will know, one way or the other.`

**27 — The Long Saturday** · Sat 17 Oct
*Object:* a business plan, forty-one pages, and a decision.
*Whose life:* all of them. The society is formed; the lease goes to committee with an officer's recommendation to grant; it is not a triumph, it is a stage of a process, and the traders cheer anyway because you take what you are given.
*What shifts:* nobody rescues anybody. Forty people do a boring thing correctly and the hall stands. Alison's name is on page two as a founder member and she looks at it for a while. Hilary takes the three keys that open nothing off the ring and puts them in a drawer.
*Closing image:* Bernie giving out pies to a hall full of people at four in the afternoon and refusing, on principle, every single coin.
*Cliffhanger:* `The clocks go back a week on Sunday, and she has four yards of cloth to buy.`

**28 — Four Yards** · Tue 20 Oct
*Object:* four yards of wool, bought from a mill shop in Todmorden on a Tuesday morning, in a colour she has to stand in the doorway with to be sure of.
*Whose life:* hers. Entirely hers, for the first time in the series.
*What shifts:* she buys cloth for herself. She is fifty-two. It costs eighty-four pounds and she pays it out of the redundancy and does not do any division on the back of any envelope.
*Closing image:* the bolt on the counter and the shop woman cutting it, and Alison saying, before she can stop herself, *leave me a bit extra* — and the woman, who has been cutting cloth for thirty years, not needing to ask why.
*Cliffhanger:* `She has not cut a pattern of her own since she was nineteen years old.`

**29 — Cutting** · Thu 22 Oct — ★ *this one lands*
*Object:* four yards of wool on the floor of her front room, her own tape measure, and her own body.
*Whose life:* hers. Alone. Night. This is the series' most intimate chapter and it must be as quiet as *The Long Room*'s chapter 29.
*What shifts:* she takes her own measurements. Standing in her slip in front of the wardrobe mirror at half past ten at night with a tape she has put round ten thousand other women and never once round herself. She writes the numbers down. They are not the numbers of 1993 and she writes them down anyway, without a single word of apology to herself, because a maker measures what is there. The small clear patch on her hip catches the light once, like a little window in the skin, and she notes it the way she would note a seam and moves on. She chalks the cloth on the floor on her knees, which take a moment to agree to it. She cuts. The sound of the shears — Pauline's shears, red tape on the handle — going through four yards of wool at eleven at night is the best sound in the world.
*Closing image:* the pieces of a coat laid out on the carpet in the shape of a woman, and Alison sitting back on her heels beside them, and the flat gone completely quiet, and her own hand steady.
*Cliffhanger:* `In the morning the year turns, and there is a coat with no buttons on it.`

**30 — The Coat** · Sat 24 Oct — ★ *the close. Match the weight of The Long Room's chapter 30.*
*Object:* the coat, finished at ten past two on the last Saturday of the old time, with the clocks going back at two in the morning.
*Whose life:* hers — and, because of how the series has worked, everybody's.
*What shifts:* nothing dramatic. That is the point. She sews the last button on at the table, in the hall, with the hall going on around her — Bernie's commentary, Yusuf's wheel, Margo's terrible record, Kelly's misspelled chalkboard, Sonia's brass measure, Hilary's keys, Pauline threading a needle at the Thursday table for somebody else. Nobody makes a fuss, because they are Northern and it is a Saturday. Sonia says *turn round*, once, and Alison turns round, and that is the whole ceremony.
*The closing movement (for the writer):* she thinks of the north window at Ellerby's, and the light that was never the bulb. She thinks about the masking tape with HOYLE on it, still in the pocket of a coat she no longer wears. She thinks about her body, which has altered itself these two years without a fitting, without a chalk line, without anybody asking her to stand still — and how she does not know what is on the other side of it, and nobody can tell her, because nobody else is her. What she can say, at fifty-two, standing under eleven glass lanterns in a coat she made, is this: that she has spent her whole life leaving room for other women. That she has begun, late, to leave some for herself. That she was taken in by a hall full of people who did not ask her to be anything other than good at what she is good at. That the year turns tomorrow and the light goes at four, and there is a lamp, and a Thursday, and six needles, and a class that is full until March.
*Closing image:* she walks out of the north door at five o'clock into the first real cold of the year, in her own coat, cut to her own measurements, and it fits, because she made it to fit *this* body — and she does not look at her reflection in the window of the shop opposite, and then she does, and it is all right. Behind her the lanterns are lit and the roof of the hall glows from the inside, and it will be dark at four from tomorrow, and she stands a moment in the cold with her hands in her own pockets and lets it be dark.
*Cliffhanger:* `She has cloth left over. She always leaves an inch and a half.`

### Running motifs (seed them, do not explain them)
1. **Light.** North window at Ellerby's (the light was never the bulb) → the eleven lanterns → the Argos daylight lamp → the roof glowing from inside → the clocks going back. Alison's eyes want more light for the needle. This is never once described as a symptom.
2. **Seam allowance.** The inch and a half a maker leaves. Introduced ch 9, named ch 15, paid off in the final line of ch 30.
3. **The chalk line.** Pauline's hand vs Alison's (ch 7, 21). And the series' governing image: *her body had begun to alter itself without a fitting — nobody chalked the line, nobody asked her to stand still.* Use at ch 15 and echo at ch 30. Do not use it more than twice.
4. **Hands.** Alison's flat thumb pad; Sonia's thumb on the shine; Yusuf's head on one side; Bernie's hand in the deeper pocket; Pauline threading everybody's needles.
5. **What things are worth.** The £830 a year; four pounds for a knee; thirty-eight for curtains; £1.60 for a brush; a pie for a pocket; eighty-four pounds of cloth bought without division.
6. **Made, not mended.** Ch 8 (the deeper pocket, unremarked) → ch 16 (asked to make, frightened) → ch 24 (mittens from a jumper) → ch 26 (relined) → ch 29–30 (the coat).

### Hard prohibitions
- **No repeats of *The Long Room*'s furniture:** no architect, no derelict building, no dead mother, no inheritance, no hidden box, no cavity in a wall, no historical secret, no archive, no canal-window flat, no pregnant daughter, no academic rivalry.
- **No body-shame beat, ever.** A garment that no longer fits is a technical problem with a generous solution. The maker left room. Nobody's body is a monument. Alison never comments on any woman's size, including her own, except to write a number down honestly in ch 29.
- **No villain, no jeopardy, no peril, no cruelty.** The council officer is decent. The silence is not a betrayal. The machine is mended for £1.60.
- **No clinical language.** No dosage, no diagnosis, no scene at a GP. One patch, one glance, ch 29, no comment.
- **Health is one room, not the house.** Across the 30: work, money, uniform poverty, career re-entry, migration, marriage, widowhood, motherhood, adult children, friendship, faith and family celebration, music, sport, identity, ageing, joy, and a community organising itself. Midlife is the weather, not the plot.
