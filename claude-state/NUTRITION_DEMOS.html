Nutrition — 5 UX demo directions

2026-06-12 · Same complete feature set, five genuinely different layouts — each built to kill decision fatigue on a feature-dense page and use richer UI than a 7-tab wall. Open each on your phone. Mock data, no entities. Live after deploy (hash logged in STATUS).
The brief, restated: the current Nutrition page is 7 tabs (Today · My Plan · Recipes · AI Plan · Shop · Progress · Insights). That's a wall of equal choices every time you arrive — classic overwhelm. Your note was right: we weren't using rich components. "A big card slider in the middle solves a lot — you can do a lot on one line." So each demo below keeps ALL the features but reorganises them around ONE strong UX metaphor, so at any moment there's one obvious thing in front of you.

1 · Hero Card Slider /NutritionDemo1

1Hero Card Slider

A big swipeable card slider is the spine of the page.

MetaphorThe 7-tab bar is gone. A horizontal scroll-snap slider sits in the middle — each of the 8 surfaces is one tall, rich card, with the next card peeking at the right edge so you know there's more. Dots + a label rail tell you where you are; each card carries exactly one primary action.
Decision fatigue it killsCollapses eight choices into one gesture — at any moment there's one card in front of you and one obvious action, instead of seven tabs competing up-front.
GoodDirectly your idea; rich and modern; one-thing-at-a-time; the peek invites exploration without pressure.
Trade-offWeaker random access — to reach Shop or Insights you swipe past the others or use the label rail; each card is a fixed height, so the longest content (full meal plan, micro list) scrolls within the card.

2 · Daily Hub /NutritionDemo2

2Daily Hub

One calm screen, one action — the rest in bottom sheets.

MetaphorHome is a single quiet screen: a "today's plate" ring, Jess's one line, a single "Log a meal" button, and exactly ONE suggested next action (tonight's dinner). The other surfaces are minimal tiles in a 2-column grid that slide up as bottom sheets.
Decision fatigue it killsAnswers "what do I do now?" with one obvious action on load. The screen is never a wall of tabs — depth is one calm tap away.
GoodThe calmest landing; strong "one primary action" discipline; bottom sheets are native and familiar; great for a quick daily glance.
Trade-offEverything beyond the hub is one extra tap and never glanceable together — scanning your whole picture means opening/closing sheets one by one.

3 · Log-First /NutritionDemo3

3Log-First

A persistent "+ Log" button and a rich quick-add sheet.

MetaphorBuilt around the one frequent job — logging in seconds. A pinned "+ Log" (plus an inline hero) opens a segmented sheet: Search / Recents / Favourites / Photo / Voice / Barcode. A slim "today" strip sits above; everything else (Plan, Recipes, Meal Plan, Shop, Progress, Insights) lives behind one quiet "Everything else" menu.
Decision fatigue it killsMakes the #1 action a single thumb-tap and demotes the rest to progressive disclosure, so the screen is never busy.
GoodFastest possible logging; honest about what people do most; the quick-add sheet (recents/favourites) is the real friction-killer from the plan.
Trade-offOptimises hard for the logger at the explorer's expense — someone who loves recipes/planning has to dig through "Everything else" to find the app's richest features.

4 · Day-as-a-Story Timeline /NutritionDemo4

4Day-as-a-Story Timeline

One vertical scroll — your day, narrated. No tabs at all.

MetaphorThe whole page is one calm top-to-bottom scroll. Meals sit as time-stamped entries on a timeline spine; between them Jess weaves a morning greeting, a hydration nudge, the "afternoons run light" pattern, an iron note and an evening recipe. Plan / Week / Insights unfold as script-titled section-breaks. A slim sticky rail (Morning/Plan/Week/Insights) + a floating "+ Log" are the only navigation.
Decision fatigue it killsRemoves navigation entirely — you never choose a section, you scroll your own day in a calm, fixed narrative order.
GoodThe warmest, most whole-life and least "tracker" feel; Jess as a companion really lands; reads beautifully in our editorial type.
Trade-offDirect, repeated access (jump straight to the shopping list mid-cook) is deeper down; the jump-rail + floating Log mitigate it, but it trades fast random-access for a guided narrative.
single feednarrative orderzero navigation

5Editorial Magazine

Swipeable full-bleed spreads you read one at a time.

MetaphorFive immersive, one-per-screen spreads — The Plate · The Kitchen · The Pantry · The Body · The Week — each a finished magazine page with a cover block, a big Ephesis script title, strong type hierarchy and exactly one CTA. A segmented control + page dots turn the pages.
Decision fatigue it killsAt any moment you're reading one finished, rich page with a single action — never scanning a grid of competing cards and controls.
GoodThe most premium, brand-forward direction; makes nutrition feel aspirational, not clinical; showcases the Ephesis/Cormorant identity at its best.
Trade-offOne-spread-per-screen costs cross-surface comparison — checking today's iron against your meal plan and shopping list means paging between three spreads rather than seeing them together.
magazine spreadssegmented controlimmersive

What every demo contains (the full feature set)

  • Today's plate — gentle energy + macro picture (a guide, never a calorie scoreboard) + hydration.
  • Frictionless logging — recents, favourites, photo, voice, barcode, food search (the P0/P1 logging layer).
  • My Plan — soft targets framed as "what your body's asking for" (energy + the 5 women's micronutrients).
  • Recipes — saved recipes + an AI generator that cooks from what you have in.
  • AI Meal Plan — a gentle, stage-aware week (weekends left open on purpose).
  • Shop — the shopping list grouped by aisle, with a rough £ estimate.
  • Progress — gentle patterns over time. No streaks, no scores, no vanity counts.
  • Insights — women's stage-aware micronutrient nudges (iron · fibre · calcium · protein · folate) in Jess's voice.
Locked constraints held across all five: cream/ink palette, Ephesis + Cormorant only, Lucide/SVG icons, no emoji, no scoreboards, whole-life not clinical, Jess present. Mock data only; useState only; no entities queried — safe to ship alongside production.

Why these patterns — cited research

Modern mobile UX patterns that reduce decision fatigue on feature-dense screens. The five demos each lead with one of these.

0 · The principle — Hick's Law / choice overload. Decision time rises with the number and complexity of simultaneous choices; cutting concurrent options is the root mechanism behind every pattern here.
Laws of UX, "Hick's Law" (lawsofux.com/hicks-law) · NN/g, "Simplicity Wins over Abundance of Choice" — an excess of choices leads to fatigue or abandonment.

1 · Horizontal card sliders with peek + scroll-snap (→ Demo 1). Half-cut next cards are a strong cue that more lies sideways; scroll-snap clicks each into place.
NN/g, "Carousels on Mobile Devices" · web.dev, "Well-controlled scrolling with CSS Scroll Snap." Risk: horizontally-hidden content has lower discovery — never hide crucial info there.

2 · Bottom sheets for quick actions / progressive disclosure (→ Demos 2 & 3). A partial overlay summons detail on demand and keeps the base screen sparse.
NN/g, "Bottom Sheets: Definition and UX Guidelines" (a form of progressive disclosure) · Apple HIG, "Sheets." Risk: hidden content is less discoverable; don't nest navigation inside.

3 · Hub-and-spoke + "one primary action per screen" (→ Demo 2). A central hub branches to focused, low-choice tasks.
NN/g, "Basic Patterns for Mobile Navigation." Risk: costly for users who switch sections often (constant return-to-hub).

4 · Progressive disclosure / staged reveal (→ Demos 3 & 4). Show only what the current step needs; defer the rest.
NN/g, "Minimize Cognitive Load to Maximize Usability" (Nielsen, orig. 1995). Risk: over-disclosure buries features users want up-front.

5 · Single-feed / timeline-as-narrative (→ Demo 4). Consolidate sections into one ordered scroll; imposing sequence lowers cognitive load even when not required.
NN/g, "Minimize Cognitive Load" — "impose sequence… to reduce cognitive load." Risk: long feeds hurt random access; needs anchors/sticky markers.

6 · Segmented controls (2–5 items) (→ Demo 5). Best for a small set of mutually-exclusive views swapped in place.
Material Design 3, "Segmented button — Guidelines" (2–5 options) · Apple HIG, "Tab bars" (3–5 persistent destinations). Risk: doesn't scale past ~5 — a 7-way segment reproduces the 7-tab overwhelm.

7 · Peek/snap affordances & the "illusion of completeness" (→ Demos 1 & 5). A visible peeking edge signals "more this way"; if nothing peeks, users assume the screen is complete and never find the rest.
web.dev / MDN, CSS Scroll Snap (scroll-padding "sneak-peek") · NN/g, "The Illusion of Completeness."

8 · Chunking & grouping (the connective tissue). NN/g pairs grouping with Hick's Law — the eye reads 3–4 labelled clusters far faster than 11 flat items.
NN/g, "Hick's Law: Designing Long Menu Lists."
